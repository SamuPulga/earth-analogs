Temperature

From oven to freezer

With no atmosphere to hold heat, the surface temperature swings to extremes between day and night: near the equator it goes from about 120 °C in sunlight to about −130 °C at night.

At the south pole the Sun always sits very low on the horizon, so the floors of some craters never receive light. These permanently shadowed regions are among the coldest places measured in the solar system, at about −240 °C according to LRO's Diviner radiometer.

  • Temperature variation: the most useful parameter for comparing with Earth, since no place on Earth reaches those absolute values.
  • Humidity and wind: do not apply. The Moon has no atmosphere, so the app turns them off for lunar destinations.

Surface

Regolith, craters and little reflected light

The surface is covered in regolith: dust and rock fragments produced by millions of years of impacts. It is fine, abrasive and clings to everything because of electrostatic charge.

The Moon reflects only about 12 % of the light it receives, similar to asphalt. With the grazing sunlight of the south pole, shadows are long and pitch black: a challenge for a rover's navigation cameras.

  • Reflectance: basaltic lava fields are among the few surfaces on Earth as dark as regolith.

Destinations we compare against

What no place on Earth can reproduce

  • Gravity: on Earth everything weighs about 6 times more. It changes wheel traction and how an astronaut walks.
  • Vacuum: without air, heat moves only by radiation and conduction, and dust is not slowed down.
  • Radiation: Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field protect us; the Moon has neither.

Climate

Cold, dry and very dusty

At the Viking 1 site the temperature ranged from about −89 °C at night to −31 °C during the day. The air is so dry and so thin that liquid water does not last on the surface.

The wind usually blows at 2 to 10 m/s and reaches about 30 m/s in dust storms. But Martian air has only 1.6 % of Earth's density (0.020 vs. 1.217 kg/m³): at the same speed, the wind pushes far less.

  • Humidity: used to find the driest places on Earth.
  • Wind speed: useful for dust transport and deposition, not for wind loads on a structure.
  • Temperature and its variation: for thermal-cycling tests of equipment.

Surface

Iron oxide and ancient lakes

The reddish color comes from iron oxide in the dust. The surface has giant volcanoes such as Olympus Mons, the Valles Marineris canyon, and craters that once held water, such as Jezero.

  • Reflectance: Mars reflects more light than the Moon (albedo ~0.25), similar to many reddish deserts on Earth.

Destinations we compare against

What no place on Earth can reproduce

  • Gravity: on Earth everything weighs about 2.6 times more than on Mars.
  • Thin CO₂ atmosphere: Earth's pressure is about 160 times higher. It changes how heat moves and how much force the wind exerts.
  • Radiation: Mars has almost no magnetic field and its atmosphere filters little radiation.

          05 · Science

          The science behind it

          What a terrestrial analog is, why these parameters, the mathematics that ranks the places, and the limits of the method.

          The science: what a terrestrial analog is

          A terrestrial analog is a place on Earth whose conditions resemble those of another world in some measurable dimensions, so that equipment, procedures and people can be tested there before flying. NASA and other agencies have used analogs since the Apollo era: impact craters and lava fields to train astronauts in geology, polar and desert sites to test rovers, habitats and instruments.

          No place on Earth reproduces the Moon or Mars as a whole: gravity, vacuum or a very thin atmosphere, and the radiation environment cannot be recreated in the field. An analog is therefore always partial, and its value depends on what the mission needs to test. That is the idea behind S.E.I.A.T: instead of a single "similarity" score, each mission defines which parameters matter and how much, and the model finds the places that best match those priorities.

          Scope: the Americas, from the Canadian Arctic to Patagonia. The continent spans polar deserts, hyper-arid deserts, high plateaus, volcanic fields and salt flats, enough contrast to cover the missions while keeping the data volume manageable.

          Why these five parameters

          Surface temperature

          Sets the thermal design of every system: batteries, electronics, lubricants, seals and materials must work in the cold of the target world.

          Moon: about −173 °C to +127 °C at the equator, far colder in polar shadows · Mars: mean about −63 °C.

          Day–night temperature range

          Repeated heating and cooling causes thermal fatigue in joints, solar panels and structures. Large daily swings are what thermal-cycling tests look for.

          Moon: about 300 °C between day and night at the equator · Mars: about 60 °C at the Viking 1 site (−89 °C to −31 °C).

          Relative humidity

          Water vapour changes how dust sticks and clumps, corrodes metals and affects electronics. Mars is extremely dry; the driest places on Earth are the closest match.

          Moon: no atmosphere (not applicable) · Mars: water vapour about 0.02 % of the atmosphere.

          Wind speed

          Wind lifts, carries and deposits dust, abrades surfaces and covers solar panels. On Mars the air is about 1 % as dense as on Earth, so the same speed pushes far less: wind is an analog for dust transport, not for wind loads.

          Moon: no atmosphere (not applicable) · Mars: about 2–10 m/s at the Viking sites, 17–30 m/s in dust storms.

          Surface albedo

          How bright the ground is controls the light that cameras and navigation sensors see, and how much sunlight the surface absorbs. A rover's vision system trained on a dark, regolith-like surface behaves more like it will on the Moon.

          Moon: geometric albedo about 0.12 · Mars: Bond albedo about 0.25.

          Reference values (rounded) from the NASA NSSDCA Moon and Mars Fact Sheets and LRO Diviner. They describe each world, not the exact destination site.

          Destination targets

          For each destination, the value each parameter tries to reproduce. When the real value lies beyond anything that exists on Earth (the Moon is colder than any place in the Americas), the target is the most extreme value found on the continent: the closest Earth can get. In Plan a mission, each target can be changed; values outside the range of the Americas are clipped to its minimum or maximum.

          The mathematical model

          Choosing an analog is a multi-objective optimization problem: we want to be close to the target in several parameters at once, and those goals compete (the coldest places in the Americas are also humid). The model works in four steps.

          1. 01

            Normalized gap

            For each grid point x and parameter i, the distance to the target vi is divided by the range of that parameter in the Americas, so every parameter is unitless and comparable:

            fi(x) = |xi − vi| / (maxi − mini)  ∈ [0, 1]

            0 means identical to the target; 1 means as far as the whole continental range. A target outside the range is first clipped to it: vi = min(max(vi, mini), maxi).

          2. 02

            Pareto front

            Point a dominates point b if it is at least as close to the target in every parameter and strictly closer in at least one:

            a ≻ b  ⇔  fi(a) ≤ fi(b) ∀i  and  ∃j: fj(a) < fj(b)

            The Pareto front is the set of points that no other point dominates. A dominated point is never recommended: another place beats it in everything. Computing it compares every pair of points, O(k·N²) with N = 3,857 points.

          3. 03

            Weighted Chebyshev

            The mission's weights wi (normalized so that Σwi = 1) decide which points of the front are best. Each point is scored by its worst weighted gap:

            s(x) = maxi wi · fi(x)   →   optimal site = argminx s(x)

            The parameter that sets the maximum is the decisive parameter: it is what keeps that place from being a better match. Unlike a weighted sum, every point of the Pareto front can be the optimum for some choice of weights (Steuer & Choo, 1983; Miettinen, 1999), so balanced places are never hidden.

          4. 04

            Robustness

            Weights are judgements, so the result must not hinge on their exact value. The calculation is repeated 500 times with each weight perturbed at random:

            w̃i = wi · eε,    ε ~ N(0, 0.2²)

            Robustness is the share of those runs in which a place comes first. A high value means the recommendation holds even if the weights are somewhat off. The random seed is fixed, so every result is reproducible.

          Why Chebyshev and not an average

          Don't hide the balanced sites

          With five sites A(1, 8), B(2, 5), C(4.2, 4.2), D(5, 2) and E(8, 1), a weighted sum never picks C, with any weights, because C lies in a "sunken" (non-convex) part of the front (Das & Dennis, 1997).

          Chebyshev with equal weights does pick it: its worst gap is 4.2, versus 5 for B and D. A site that is acceptable in everything should not lose to one that is excellent in one thing and poor in another.

          Reading a result

          Gap per parameter

          Shown as a percentage of the range in the Americas: up to 10 % is a close match, up to 25 % partial, above 25 % far.

          Best compromise

          If even the optimal site is far from some target, no place in the Americas reproduces that combination. The app says so instead of presenting it as a full analog.

          Positive control

          Places already used by NASA and field teams (Haughton Crater, Atacama, Meteor Crater…) should rank high when the settings describe the mission well. Their rank is shown as a check of the method.

          The measured data

          The Americas are divided into land cells of 1° × 1° (about 110 km on a side at the equator). Each cell holds the five surface parameters; only cells with data for all five are compared.

          The parameters by world

          Data products for each body. Cells marked "not applicable" are turned off automatically in the app.

          Limits of the method

          • Resolution. A 1° cell averages coast and mountains, valleys and peaks. The result points to a region; a field test needs a site survey inside it.
          • Surface, not everything. The five parameters describe climate and surface brightness. Slope, rock abundance, soil texture, mineralogy and ice are not in the model yet.
          • Earth's limits. No place reproduces lunar or Martian gravity, vacuum or the thin Martian atmosphere, nor the radiation environment. Every recommendation is a partial analog.
          • Weights are choices. They express what a mission prioritizes. Robustness shows how much the result depends on them.

          Use of artificial intelligence

          Artificial intelligence is used in two places, and never to choose a site. (1) It writes the profile of a place from open data (OpenStreetMap, Wikipedia, ERA5 climate) and from the model's own numbers, and is instructed to state only facts present in that data. (2) In Plan a mission, it can translate a mission described in plain language into targets and weights; the app validates that proposal and the user can edit it. The ranking always comes from the mathematical model. Model: openai/gpt-oss-120b via Groq.

          06 · Who it's for

          Built for the people who test before flying

          Anyone who needs to choose where on Earth to test a rover, an instrument, a habitat or a procedure meant for the Moon or Mars, with a method that is systematic, transparent and reproducible.

          01

          Universities and student teams

          Rover competitions, CubeSat and instrument projects, and research groups that need a field site but cannot afford trial and error.

          Example: a student rover team looks for a dry, windy place in the Americas to see how dust covers its solar panels.

          02

          Space start-ups and industry

          Companies developing rovers, landers, drills, power systems or sensors that must prove their hardware in conditions close to the target world.

          Example: a start-up checks where its camera-based navigation will face ground as dark as lunar regolith and strong day–night cycles.

          03

          Analog research programs

          Field stations and analog missions that want to justify a site with data, or discover new candidate sites beyond the famous ones.

          Example: a program compares where a new site ranks against Haughton Crater or the Atacama for the same mission.

          04

          Space agencies and mission planners

          Teams that plan test campaigns for Artemis-era lunar systems or Mars hardware and need a transparent first screening of candidate regions.

          Example: a planner explores which regions keep their rank when the weights of the mission change (robustness).

          05

          Teachers and science communicators

          A visual way to explain what makes the Moon and Mars different from Earth, and why no single place on Earth can reproduce them.

          Example: a class colors the map by day–night temperature range and discusses why deserts and high plateaus stand out.

          What you get

          A ranked list of places

          The best places in the Americas for your mission, on a map, with the optimal site highlighted and how robust its first place is.

          The reasons, with numbers

          For each place: its measured values against the targets, the parameters that match and the ones that do not, and an AI-written profile built only from data.

          Honest limits

          What Earth cannot reproduce (gravity, vacuum, radiation) and a clear warning when no place gets close to every target.

          06

          Try it with your mission

          Pick a preset mission, set your own targets and weights, or describe it in your own words.

          07 · How it works

          From a mission to a place, in six steps

          A short guide to get a recommendation and understand it. Each step has a link to try it.

          1. 1

            Choose your mission

            Open Plan a mission. You have three ways to start:

            • Preset: three ready-made missions (lunar rover navigation, thermal testing for Mars, structures against Martian dust).
            • Custom: start from a destination and add the parameters you need.
            • AI assistant: describe the mission in your own words; the AI translates it into targets and weights that you can review.
            Open Plan a mission
          2. 2

            Set a target and a weight for each parameter

            The target is the value you want to reproduce; by default it is the destination's value. When the Moon or Mars is beyond anything on Earth, the target is the most extreme value in the Americas. If you type a value outside what exists on the continent, it is clipped to the maximum or minimum.

            The weight (1 to 100) says how much each parameter matters. Only the proportions count: the bar under each parameter shows its share of the total.

          3. 3

            Read the map and the ranking

            Every 1° square of the Americas is colored by how well it matches your settings: bright blue = better match. The map flies to the optimal site and numbers the top 10. The ranking lists them with their score (lower = better) and the parameter that decides it.

          4. 4

            Open the site profile

            Click a place in the ranking or any square on the map. You will see:

            • its values compared with the targets, with the gap of each parameter (green close, yellow partial, red far);
            • where it is, its climate and a Wikipedia summary of the area;
            • an AI-written argument of why it is an analog for your mission, what you could test there and where it falls short;
            • the measured data maps of your mission's parameters.
          5. 5

            Check how good the result is

            • Best compromise: a yellow warning appears when even the optimal site is far from some target. Lower the weight of what matters less, or accept that Earth cannot reproduce it.
            • Robustness: how often the place stays first when the weights change a little. Low robustness means you should also look at the next places.
            • Positive control: where well-known analogs (Haughton, Atacama, Meteor Crater…) rank with your settings.
          6. 6

            Explore on your own

            In Analogs, color the whole map by a measured parameter or by a preset mission, and click any square to see its values and its rank in every mission. In Science you will find the science and mathematics behind the method.

            Open Analogs Read the science

          Tips

          Fewer parameters, clearer answers

          Two or three parameters give a sharper ranking. With many at once, almost every place becomes a reasonable compromise.

          The decisive parameter

          It is the one that limits the score. If it is not what your mission cares about, lower its weight and watch the ranking change.

          A region, not a spot

          Each result is a 1° square (about 110 km). Use it to choose a region, then survey sites inside it.
